# How we found it (contributor text, unverified)

Twelve deep-moonquake nests could be stacked on Apollo S14 (MH1/MH2) and S16 (MH1/MH2) from their own catalogued 1973
events (whitened, 0.25-1.0 Hz, 450 s windows). All of 1973 was scanned with a two-station coincidence statistic that
lets each station take its best lag within 2.1 s (the bank's moveout tolerance), and each nest was tested against its
own 401 controls (time-reversed data, and S16 circularly shifted by 1-21 h). All of this was pre-registered before
the scan. The strongest uncatalogued detection matched the A100 stack.

A first panel left it unconfirmed for two reasons. One published S16 event list (Knapmeyer-Endrun and Hammer 2015) could
not be read (the publisher answered 403); the human downloaded it by hand and it has no event near this time. The
second reason was that the S16 MH2 channel showed nothing while every catalogued A100 detection had shown a strong
match there. A second pre-registration then fixed tests that could take the attribution away. They checked all 12 stacks,
the lag profile, the Nakamura 2005 moveouts, and S16 MH2's response to every catalogued event that day. They also ran
a reverse search, scanning the event's own waveform over all of 1973. The S16 MH2 doubt turned out to be the
instrument: on 1973-11-25 to 11-27, S16 MH2 did not respond to any catalogued deep moonquake, including large A1, A20,
A33 and A203 events. The original analysts' trace flags for those days mark LPX but not LPY. The reverse search ranked
catalogued A100 events first to sixth of 998 deep moonquakes of 1973 (first to fifth in the REPRODUCE panelist's
independent code). The event also sits on A100's monthly 1973
sequence. The published acceleration-glitch catalogue (Liu, Xiao and Li 2025), which covers S16 too, has no entry in
either station's window.
