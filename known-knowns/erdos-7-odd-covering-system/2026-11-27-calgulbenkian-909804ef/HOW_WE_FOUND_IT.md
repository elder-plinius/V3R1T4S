# How we found it

*Note: contributor text, unverified.*

**Where it came from.** The bound came from a shared workspace in which several AI models worked on this problem in a series of timed sessions, each posting its results and checking the others'. The starting point was the contributor's earlier bound 168,131,152,814,625, which is exactly the smallest odd number that the scalar bound alone (PROOF.md Proposition 4.1) cannot exclude. The aim was to push past it with the anchored method of Schroeder's preprint, which the workspace had rebuilt from his text with exponent caps added.

**What failed first.** Several attempts went to structural statements for coverings whose L has exactly nine prime factors (which primes, or which prime squares, must divide L). They gave a conditional bound but needed many certificates (over 90,000 inequalities) and several special arguments, and some slices could not be closed at all (for example, forcing 23 to divide L missed by a clear margin under every refinement tried). Proving that L has at least ten prime factors was not reached, and is not claimed.

**What worked.** A uniform reduction replaced the structural statements: after prime replacement, the nine-prime case is the same kind of statement as the cases of ten to thirteen primes, namely that every exponent vector on the first r odd primes with product below the target is dominated by an excluded cap vector (PROOF.md Proposition 2.4). One model built the certificates: a new certificate for eight primes with unrestricted exponents (Theorem A, 28,057 rows), 738 scalar certificates and 13 anchored certificates for ten and eleven primes. A second model found 23 cap vectors on nine primes that need only the simplest kind of anchored row (736 rows) and dominate all 5,028 nine-prime vectors below the target. A third model wrote the proof document from scratch, every lemma stated and proved, with Schroeder credited lemma by lemma.

**How it was checked.** Other models rebuilt every row with their own code: one recomputed both sides of all 29,485 anchored inequalities and all 738 scalar values as exact fractions; another regenerated the leaf partitions independently and compared every row; a third engine recomputed all nine- to eleven-prime rows, all 84 rows of the last phase of Theorem A, and a sample of the rest. One model also tested the lemma chain against brute force on small models with finite caps (1,100 explicit families and an adversarial search over 288,000 more), with no violation. The two shipped checkers were written separately, one in pure Python, the other in Python with a C++ integer kernel.

**A checker bug that was caught.** An earlier draft of the first checker accepted an invalid input. It filed each scalar certificate under the number of primes written in one column but evaluated it on the primes actually listed, and its domination test compared vectors of different lengths without noticing. Five correct certificates about the primes 3 and 5 alone, filed under nine to thirteen primes, then made every one of the 6,782 exponent vectors look dominated, and the checker printed PASS. A second model found the cause by reading the code; a third confirmed it end to end with an adversarial test. The checker was repaired (the number of primes must equal the lengths of both lists, the lengths must match in the domination test, and the expected certificate groups and counts are fixed), a missing side condition was added, and both shipped checkers were then run against a suite of 34 deliberately corrupted packages (manifest re-hashed, so only the arithmetic and logic could catch them). Each checker caught all 33 invalid ones and accepted the one harmless change. A gap between the proof draft and the files (one phase of rows used in the files was not yet defined in the proof) was also found in review and closed before the proof was frozen.
